    The Architecture of Everyday Life: Rethinking Residential Floor Plans

    By Elena Marsh · March 14, 2025 · 18 min read

    For decades, the open-plan home has been the default. But as we spend more time in our houses, a quiet counter-movement is taking shape — one that values enclosure, nuance, and the ability to close a door. This isn't about returning to stuffy corridors; it's about designing for the full spectrum of domestic life.

    The problem with pure openness

    Open plan solved a real problem: it freed us from the cramped, cell-like rooms of the postwar era. But it created another — a lack of acoustic and visual privacy. In a fully open living space, the sound of a boiling kettle travels to the farthest bedroom. A pile of toys in the corner is visible from every seat. And the kitchen, once a place of retreat, becomes a stage.

    “A home should offer choices. Some days you want to be together; some days you need to be apart — without leaving the building.”

    Three spatial moves that restore balance

    1. The pocket room. A small, enclosed room — no larger than 8 square metres — that can be used as a study, a reading nook, or a guest space. It doesn't need a corridor; it can open directly off the main living area with a sliding door.

    2. The half-wall. A waist-height partition gives visual separation without closing off light. It can double as a bookshelf, a desk, or a dining bench.

    3. The zoned ceiling. Changing ceiling height — dropping it over the kitchen, raising it over the living area — creates psychological boundaries without physical walls.

    Circulation: the unsung hero

    Good circulation is invisible. You don't notice it when it's right, but you feel it when it's wrong. Aim for a minimum of 900mm for main pathways and 750mm for secondary routes. Avoid dead ends. And remember: a corridor doesn't have to be a corridor — it can be a gallery, a library, or a place to sit.

    In the end, the best floor plan is one that adapts. It gives you room to breathe, to gather, and to retreat — without asking you to choose one mode of living over another.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What's the most common mistake in interior design? +
    Scale. Too many people choose furniture that's either too large or too small for the room. Measure twice, and consider how the piece will relate to the architecture — not just the empty floor space.
    How do I choose a paint colour that will actually look good? +
    Always test it in the room, on multiple walls, at different times of day. Undertones matter more than the name on the label. And remember: north-facing rooms need warmer colours; south-facing rooms can handle cooler ones.
    Is sustainable design more expensive? +
    Not necessarily. Reusing existing materials, choosing local timber, and designing for passive heating and cooling can reduce costs over time. The upfront premium for some green materials is falling fast.
    How do I make a small room feel bigger? +
    Maximise natural light, use a consistent floor material throughout, and keep sightlines clear. Mirrors help, but they're not a substitute for good spatial planning. Avoid heavy curtains and bulky furniture.
